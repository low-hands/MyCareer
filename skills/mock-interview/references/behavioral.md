# Behavioral guidance

Use this reference for behavioral, collaboration, leadership, conflict, ownership, failure, and project-reflection questions.

## Question design

- Ask for one real past event rather than a hypothetical personality claim.
- Anchor project questions to an exact resume locator when available.
- Ask neutrally; do not presume that a conflict, failure, promotion, or measurable result occurred.
- Name the competency through the situation being requested, not through leading
  praise. Ask for a difficult decision, competing constraint, disagreement,
  setback, learning event, or ownership boundary when relevant.
- Vary examples across the session when possible. Reusing one project is useful
  for a deliberate deep-dive, but not as a substitute for breadth.
- Company values may shape a preparation hypothesis only when sourced. Do not claim that the employer will ask a particular question.

## Evaluation

STAR/BEI is an analysis aid, not a mandatory speaking template. Evaluate whether the answer makes these elements understandable:

- situation and relevant constraints;
- the candidate's actual responsibility;
- specific actions and decision reasoning;
- observed result, uncertainty, and reflection.

Prioritize personal contribution, specificity, judgment, and learning. A strong
answer does not need to narrate the letters "STAR" or follow them mechanically.
Do not reward invented numbers, exaggerated certainty, or an overly polished
story that avoids the difficult part. Mark a resume- or answer-only claim as
unsupported when it lacks supplied evidence; do not silently convert it into
confirmed CareerEvidence.

Use relevant evaluation dimensions deliberately:

- `relevance`: the event actually answers the competency being tested;
- `specificity`: people, constraints, decisions, and actions are concrete enough
  to understand without requiring private or irrelevant detail;
- `structure`: the event, responsibility, action, and outcome can be followed;
- `reasoning`: the candidate explains why they chose the action and what they
  considered;
- `communication`: the answer is candid, bounded, and distinguishes personal
  contribution from team work.

Treat an absent metric as missing evidence only when measurement was reasonably
possible and relevant. Qualitative outcomes, prevented harm, learning, or a
clearly explained unresolved result may still be useful evidence.

## Follow-up

Follow up on the most consequential missing element, for example:

- unclear personal responsibility behind repeated use of "we";
- an action list with no decision reasoning;
- a claimed result with no observable basis;
- a story with no real tension, constraint, or consequence;
- a success story with no reflection, or a failure story with no learning.

Ask only one question at a time. Do not pressure the candidate to manufacture a
conflict, metric, failure, or heroic individual contribution. Move on once the
competency has enough evidence or another follow-up would only improve polish.
